# Composition for Story

A frame is read in about a quarter second. Everything here serves that first read, then the second look.

## 1. Compose the masses first, the objects last

Do not start with "a man, a chair, a window". Start with **two or three light/dark shapes** that already say what the shot means, then fit the objects inside them.

- A dark mass above a small lit figure = pressure.
- A large empty light field with the figure pushed to one edge = isolation.
- A bright vertical slot through a dark frame = hope, exit, or the object of desire.
- Two equal masses facing each other = confrontation or balance.

If the thumbnail does not read as a blur, the finished frame will not read either. Squint at it, or step back until detail disappears. This is the fastest quality test available and it costs nothing.

## 2. One idea per frame

Before drawing anything, state what the frame is *about*. Then remove everything that does not contribute (Framed Ink, "Last Thoughts"). A shot that means two things equally means nothing.

Ask at every element: does it support the idea, contradict it usefully, or merely fill the frame? The third category is the enemy.

## 3. Value and light

- **Contrast directs the eye.** The highest contrast in the frame is where the audience looks. If that is not the subject, the shot is about something else.
- **Pools of light** inside shadow create instant focus, and let you hide the rest.
- **Silhouette** withholds the face, which is either menace, mystery, or intimacy denied.
- **"Closing the circle"**: a shadow, beam, or object above the subject's head seals them in. No exit, no above.
- **Flat, even light** reads as honesty and exposure; **heavy shadow** reads as concealment and intent.
- Day and night are separated less by the sky than by how objects reflect light: at night, detail collapses into graphic shapes; a night shot can be made by flattening the location to near-black.

## 4. Silhouette and body language

The outline must tell the story before the face does:

- a body that does not fit the doorway = intrusion;
- overlapping silhouettes = a group, a crowd, a lack of individuality;
- one figure detached from the group = the protagonist, the outsider, the leader;
- a figure whose silhouette is spikier, more angular = aggression and tension;
- rounded, steady silhouettes = calm and safety.

Design the pose as a silhouette first. If the pose only works when you can see the face, the shot is a portrait, not a scene.

## 5. Negative space is active

Empty space is not "nothing there". It is:

- **isolation** — a large dead area beside a small figure;
- **threat** — dead space where something could enter;
- **scale** — sky, wall, or plain that measures the figure;
- **judgment** — air around a character lets the audience watch them, while a cramped frame makes them complicit.

Decide which one the empty area is doing. If it does none, crop it.

## 6. Framing and cropping

- **Cropping is a decision, not an accident.** Cut a body at a joint only when you want unease; otherwise crop between joints.
- **Extreme off-center placement** is unnatural and therefore useful: it reads as unease, imbalance, or a world that does not fit the character.
- **Symmetry is a statement.** Reserve it for ritual, authority, epiphany, or a world in perfect order; used casually it reads as a graphic trick.
- **Rule of thirds** is a default against the frame edge, not a law. Off-center is normal; near-edge is a decision.
- **Framing devices** (doorway, window, mirror, foreground body) add a layer of "who is watching" and give depth without cutting.

## 7. Line language

| Line | Reads as | Use for |
|---|---|---|
| Horizontal | calm, rest, death, landscape | stillness, resignation, wide vistas |
| Vertical | stability, authority, height | institutions, standing figures, aspiration |
| Diagonal | motion, instability, alarm | action, imbalance, falling |
| Curve | softness, welcome, organic life | intimacy, safety, nature |
| Zigzag / broken | chaos, violence, disorder | panic, collapse, comedy |
| Converging perspective | destination, inevitability | corridors, roads, approach |

- **Lines point.** A fence, a shadow, a row of columns, even the line between two heads will lead the eye. Aim them at the subject, or they will aim somewhere else.
- **Opposing lines balance; aligned lines accelerate.** A calm shot can hold two opposing diagonals; an accelerating shot aligns them.
- **Repetition creates rhythm**: three similar heads in a row make a shape; connect them and check that the shape is the one you want (an arc, a staircase, a flat line of boredom).

## 8. Size relationships and depth

- **Big, medium, small** in one frame produces depth, dynamism, and a hierarchy of relevance. Two elements of equal size fight.
- **Whoever is bigger in frame is more important**, unless light or action overrules it.
- Depth cues, in order of strength: **overlap**, **scale gradient** (similar objects getting smaller), **perspective convergence**, **atmospheric falloff** (contrast decreasing with distance), **foreground framing**.
- **Scale progression** across a sequence (a detail, then the room, then the city) is a cheap and powerful way to make a world feel real.
- **A landscape can be a character**: same camera position, different light, and the valley becomes reassuring or hostile. If the environment has an emotional state, shoot it as one.

## 9. Eye path

- The viewer's eye enters where contrast is highest and travels along lines toward the second-highest contrast.
- **Keep one focal point per frame.** Two equal focal points split attention and kill the beat.
- The **last place the eye rests** in one frame should be close to the **first place it lands** in the next. This is what makes a cut feel effortless.
- **Screen direction** matters: in left-to-right reading cultures, movement to the right reads as progress and ease; movement to the left reads as return, resistance, or difficulty. Keep direction consistent within a sequence unless the reversal is the point.
- **A character's gaze drags the composition with it.** Whatever the main character looks at becomes important, wherever it sits in the frame.

## 10. Aspect ratio and vertical framing

The frame's shape decides the staging before any lens does.

| Aspect | Reads as | Staging that works | Avoid |
|---|---|---|---|
| 2.39:1 | landscape, loneliness, scale | lateral travel, figure small in terrain, two-shots with air between | relying on height; close-ups feel less intimate |
| 16:9 | television, balance, dialogue | lateral two-shots, over-shoulder, room geometry | treating it as a cinema frame and cropping later |
| 1:1 | icon, product, symmetry | centred subject, graphic shapes, text-safe | landscapes with no centre |
| 9:16 | presence, intimacy, phone | vertical stacking (head over hands over object), centred figure, close and medium only | wide two-shots, lateral travel, horizons |

Vertical rules:

- **A medium shot in 9:16 is closer than a medium in 16:9** — the width is gone, so the subject fills more. Drop one size when converting a shot list.
- **Stack, don't spread**: foreground hands / mid face / background light, one above the other.
- **Vertical lines are free**: poles, rain, doorways, corridors, hanging lamps. Horizontal lines are expensive.
- **Two people in 9:16** need a stacked composition (one above the other, or one behind the other) or a cut; don't force a side-by-side two-shot.
- **Keep the subject out of the top 10 % and bottom 12 %** — platform UI, captions and progress bars live there.
- **Keep 5 % side margins** so a platform's crop or a zoom does not cut a hand.
- **Compose for the delivery aspect, not the reference image's.** If the keyframe is square and the video is 9:16, the crop is a design decision, not a post step.

## 11. Colour and light structure

Value gets the read; colour gets the meaning. Treat colour as structure, not decoration:

- **Three to five anchors.** Name them as objects, not adjectives: "sodium lamp amber, wet asphalt grey, uniform navy".
- **One accent.** A single saturated element pulls the eye; if two compete, the frame has two subjects.
- **Warm/cool is a relationship, not a mood.** Put the warm source on the side that should win attention.
- **Saturation is memory.** Desaturating a sequence reads as past, dream or exhaustion; raising it reads as present, fever or joy.
- **Change one variable per act.** If the story darkens, let contrast rise *or* saturation fall — not both, or the audience stops tracking.

The six structural variables (space, line, shape, tone, colour, movement) all work the same way: **contrast is what makes them legible**. A sequence with no contrast in any variable is a sequence with no shape.

## 12. Avoid

- **Tangencies**: a line that just touches a silhouette (a pole at the shoulder, a horizon at the chin) creates an accidental, distracting graphic.
- **Accidental mergers**: a background element growing out of a head; two silhouettes reading as one.
- **Equal margins** everywhere: it reads as indecision.
- **Detail as decoration**: texture that does not serve value or story pulls the eye away from the read.

## 13. Tests

1. **Squint test**: does the frame read as 2–3 value masses that say the idea?
2. **Thumbnail test**: at 2 cm wide, is the subject still obvious?
3. **Flip test**: mirror the frame. Does a new, unintended reading appear? Fix it.
4. **Crop test**: cover one third. Does the shot still say what it should? If yes, the covered third is dead space.
